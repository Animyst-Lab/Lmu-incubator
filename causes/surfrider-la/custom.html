01 — The missionWhere the land meets the sea

The ocean is not scenery. It’s the system.

Surfrider exists to protect and enjoy the world’s ocean, waves and beaches — for all people.

It does that through a grassroots activist network. The Los Angeles chapter is that network at home: volunteers who test the water, count the trash, defend the shoreline, plant gardens that catch runoff, and bring young people to their local beach.

Their coast runs from the Ballona Creek Wetlands to the Ventura County line. Plastic pollution, beach access, climate, clean water, preservation — it’s one story, because whatever touches the land eventually reaches the sea.

    03 — The work Programs · Surfrider Foundation Los Angeles

    The game ends. The work doesn’t.

    Six ongoing programs, all run with local volunteers. Open any line to see what it does — and how to join in.

    Surfrider LA hosts public beach cleanups each month at locations across Los Angeles County — education for action, community science and campaigns in one morning on the sand.

    Volunteers tally what they collect. That data helps push for upstream fixes to plastic pollution, mostly through everyday consumer choices and legislative advocacy. Cleanups alone can’t solve it, and the program says so.

    Beach cleanups ↗
    What volunteers can do
    • Join a public cleanup — see the events calendar
    • Tally as you go, so every piece becomes data
    • Companies: sponsor a plastic-free cleanup between Marina del Rey and Malibu

    Surfrider’s volunteer-run water testing, education and advocacy program. Labs measure fecal indicator bacteria in recreational water and compare it with public-health standards.

    In Los Angeles, community volunteers and Palisades High School students sample nearly 11 beaches from Marina del Rey to Malibu every other Saturday, then share the results the following Sunday — adding to the county’s own weekly monitoring.

    Blue Water Task Force ↗
    What volunteers can do
    • Join the crew of community scientists, surfers and ocean lovers
    • Email the program leads: bwtf@la.surfrider.org
    • Check local results before you surf, swim or play

    Shrinking beaches, rising seas. A volunteer-run committee monitors coastal issues, documents erosion, opposes harmful shoreline structures like seawalls, and advocates for nature-based solutions such as dune restoration.

    Local work includes being a stakeholder in the Rindge Dam removal process in Malibu, and making sure surfing is factored into plans for Topanga Lagoon.

    Coast and Climate ↗
    What volunteers can do
    • Join the coast and climate committee newsletter
    • Help document erosion and public-access problems
    • Speak up for nature-based shoreline protection

    A volunteer-run landscape education, hands-on training and advocacy program on how yards and hardscape can be reshaped to stop water pollution. The goal: reduce LA County runoff before it contaminates coastal waterways.

    Even miles inland, the trash and pollutants in your street end up in the ocean. Go organic, compost, plant natives, and shape your yard to catch and soak up rain.

    Ocean Friendly Gardens ↗
    What volunteers can do
    • Join an Ocean Friendly Garden workday
    • Start with the free beginner’s guide on the program page
    • Swap lawn for native plants at home

    Plastic is a problem long before it reaches the beach. This program recognizes restaurants that cut single-use plastic, one restaurant and one customer at a time, and builds a network that helps make the case for plastic-reduction laws.

    Seven criteria are required — including reusable foodware for dining in, no foam, no plastic takeout bags, and accessories only on request. It started in 2013 with the Huntington Beach chapter and went national in 2018.

    Ocean Friendly Restaurants ↗
    What volunteers can do
    • Recommend or sign up a restaurant you love
    • Bring your own cup, container and bag
    • Ask for straws and utensils only when you need them

    A beach access equity initiative that brings youth from underrepresented communities in LA County to their local beach for surfing lessons with partners at Aqua Surf School, time in the sun, and conversations about community watershed conservation.

    Anchored in the enjoyment half of Surfrider’s mission, it’s built on one idea: the beach belongs to everyone, regardless of who you are or where you come from. Surfrider LA reports introducing over 130 participants to surfing in its 2024 season.
